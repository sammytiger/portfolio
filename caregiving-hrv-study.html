The physiological cost of caregiving.

2011–2015Clemson · Prisma HealthPrincipal Investigatorn=32
01The Situation

Family caregivers of Alzheimer's patients absorb an enormous, largely invisible physiological load. The system tracks the patient's decline in detail; it rarely tracks the caregiver's — even though caregiver burnout is one of the most common reasons long-term home care collapses into institutional care.

02The Approach

A 3.5-year IRB-approved longitudinal study tracking heart-rate variability in 32 family caregivers, run as Principal Investigator through Clemson's Center for Success in Aging in partnership with Prisma Health. The study treated caregiver physiology as a clinical signal worth measuring in its own right — not a soft variable adjacent to the patient's chart.

03The Outcome

The findings were strong enough that seven community centers adopted a respite program built directly from the study's results — turning a research signal into a standing service. (Reported at true weight: the statistically significant HRV-coherence result comes from a within-subjects subset, n=21, p=.05.)

04From Signal to Product Decisions

The most useful finding wasn't a number — it was a sequence. Physiological load drifted measurably before caregivers reported feeling overwhelmed, and environmental changes moved that load as reliably as anything else we could offer.

That same sequence now anchors SOMA Systems as its working hypothesis: if drift precedes awareness, support has to arrive before a person knows to ask for it — quietly, with consent, and without adding a task. A decade later, it's still the product decision the study keeps making.
