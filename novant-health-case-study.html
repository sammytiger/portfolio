The Opening Question

When rural hospitals lack the staffing, infrastructure, and funding to address mental health crisis care — what does it take to build something that actually holds?

Three rural EDs. One framework. 350+ interviews. A $180K grant for the piece nobody had yet funded.

The Problem

Rural emergency departments are the de facto mental health system. They weren't designed to be.

Across Novant Health's rural North Carolina hospital network, the same pattern was repeating: patients in mental health crisis arriving in emergency departments where staff were not trained for that care, where follow-up pathways didn't exist, and where the same patients returned week after week with nowhere else to go.

The staffing shortages were real. So was the funding gap. So was the stigma — among patients, among staff, and in the communities these hospitals served. The fragmentation wasn't a single failure; it was a system that had grown up around everything except the person in crisis.

The ask was to build a Patient Experience Vision Framework — a way of seeing the problem whole, and a set of designed interventions that could be implemented with the resources actually available, not the resources a better-funded system might have.

What the Research Surfaced

Five barriers. Each one real. None of them solvable in isolation.

Synthesized from 350+ interviews across clinicians, administrators, peer recovery coaches, social workers, patients, and community members in all three hospital catchments.

01

Staffing shortages in rural areas with no pipeline to fill them.

Rural hospitals couldn't hire mental health specialists they couldn't attract or afford. Emergency physicians were absorbing psychiatric care with no training and no support.

02

No funding mechanism for peer support roles in emergency settings.

Peer recovery coaches — people with lived experience of mental health crisis — exist and are effective. But they're not billable in emergency departments under standard reimbursement structures.

03

Fragmented patient experience across care settings with no warm handoff.

Patients in crisis were discharged with a list of phone numbers and no follow-up. The moment of discharge — the most vulnerable transition point — had no designed support.

04

Stigma operating at every level — patient, staff, and community.

Patients were reluctant to self-identify. Staff were uncomfortable. Communities didn't talk about it. The silence compounded the crisis.

05

Limited tech integration for post-discharge follow-up.

Even when referrals were made, there was no system to know whether patients reached those resources — or to intervene when they didn't.

What We Built

Co-designed with the people who would use it — not for them.

The framework wasn't designed in an office and handed to the hospitals. It was built through facilitated co-design sessions with clinicians, administrators, peer recovery coaches, social workers, and patients — each with a different view of where the system broke down.

01 · Framework

Patient Experience Vision Framework

A cross-hospital framework mapping the full arc of mental health crisis care — from first contact through discharge and follow-up. Identified the highest-leverage intervention points and designed protocols that could be implemented within existing resource constraints.

02 · Peer Integration

Peer Recovery Coach Model

Designed the role, workflow, and integration model for embedding peer recovery coaches directly in emergency departments. People with lived experience of mental health crisis providing immediate, credible support at the moment of highest vulnerability.

03 · Facilitation

Multi-Site Co-Design Sessions

Led participatory design across all three hospital sites with ED physicians, nurses, social workers, peer coaches, administrators, and patient advocates. Synthesized competing priorities into a shared framework everyone could act from.

04 · Funding

Grant Strategy & Writing

Developed the funding strategy and grant narrative for behavioral health recovery program expansion. Secured $180K in grant funding to support peer recovery coach roles and program infrastructure — creating the reimbursement pathway that made the model sustainable.

Outcomes

What changed.

Measurable improvements in operations and experience. More importantly: a model that held together after the engagement ended.

ED readmissions decreased · Press Ganey scores improved
01

Seamless handoff to peer support at discharge.

Patients in crisis were no longer discharged to a phone number. The peer recovery coach model created a human connection at the transition point — the moment the system had previously left people most exposed.

02

Staff feel supported — not just patients.

Emergency department staff reported feeling less alone in managing mental health crises. The framework gave them a protocol and a colleague, not just a referral list.

03

A replicable model for rural behavioral health integration.

The funding strategy and co-design framework can be adapted for other rural hospital systems facing the same constraints. The grant narrative demonstrated that peer recovery integration in emergency settings is both clinically and financially viable.

Reflection
"The most important design decision wasn't structural — it was who was in the room when we built it."

Rural health design fails when it's done to communities rather than with them. The peer recovery coaches who ended up in those emergency departments had been in crisis themselves. They understood the experience in a way no framework could replicate — and their presence changed the dynamic not just for patients, but for clinical staff who had been quietly overwhelmed for years.

The $180K matters. The framework matters. But the lasting thing is that a group of people who had never been in the same room together — clinicians, administrators, coaches, patients — built something together they all felt ownership over. That's harder to fund and harder to measure than a readmission rate. It's also the only reason it held.
